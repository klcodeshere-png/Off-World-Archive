Apollo 11 Descent Stage

Landed 1969
Silent since 1969
Location: The Moon (Sea of Tranquility)
Coordinates: 0.674° N, 23.473° E

  1. Launch

    On 20 July 1969, the Lunar Module “Eagle” touched down with Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin aboard.

  2. Discovery

    After their moonwalk, the astronauts blasted off in the top half of Eagle, using the descent stage as a launch pad. They carried home about 21 kg of Moon rock.

  3. Final chapter

    The descent stage never moved again. It still sits in the Sea of Tranquility, and orbiting cameras have photographed it from above.

🔬 What it taught us: The crew also left a mirror-like laser reflector nearby. Scientists still bounce lasers off it to measure the Earth–Moon distance.
🚀 Space fact: A plaque on its leg reads “We came in peace for all mankind.”

Apollo 15 Lunar Rover

Landed 1971
Parked since 1971
Location: The Moon (Hadley–Apennine)
Coordinates: 26.13° N, 3.63° E (landing site)

  1. Launch

    Folded up inside Apollo 15’s lander, the first Lunar Roving Vehicle was unpacked in July 1971.

  2. Discovery

    Astronauts David Scott and James Irwin drove it about 28 km, reaching places they could never have walked to, and gathered around 77 kg of Moon rocks.

  3. Final chapter

    Scott parked it where its TV camera could watch the liftoff. It is still parked there today.

🔬 What it taught us: The rocks it helped collect revealed how the Moon’s crust formed, including the famous “Genesis Rock”.
🚀 Space fact: On this mission, Scott dropped a hammer and a feather together on the Moon, and they landed at the same time.

Opportunity Rover

2004–2018
Silent since 2018
Location: Mars (Meridiani Planum)
Coordinates: 1.95° S, 354.47° E (landing site)

  1. Launch

    Opportunity landed on 25 January 2004 and was built for a 90-day mission.

  2. Discovery

    It kept going for about 14 years and drove 45 km. It found tiny iron-rich spheres nicknamed “blueberries” and rocks that formed in water.

  3. Final chapter

    A huge dust storm blocked its sunlight in 2018. Engineers sent more than a thousand recovery commands, but it never replied. NASA ended the mission in February 2019.

🔬 What it taught us: Opportunity showed that ancient Mars had watery places that could have supported life.
🚀 Space fact: Its final data were poetically translated as “My battery is low and it’s getting dark.”

Viking 1 Lander

1976–1982
Silent since 1982
Location: Mars (Chryse Planitia)
Coordinates: 22.48° N, 47.97° W

  1. Launch

    On 20 July 1976, Viking 1 became the first NASA spacecraft to land and work on Mars.

  2. Discovery

    It sent back the first close-up pictures from the Martian surface and ran experiments to look for signs of life in the soil.

  3. Final chapter

    It worked for more than six years. In 1982 a mistaken update left its antenna pointing away from Earth, and it was never heard from again.

🔬 What it taught us: Its weather station recorded Mars’s seasons, and its soil tests gave results that scientists still debate.
🚀 Space fact: It landed exactly seven years after Apollo 11’s Moon landing.

Ingenuity Helicopter

2021–2024
Retired in 2024
Location: Mars (Jezero Crater)
Coordinates: ≈ 18.4° N, 77.5° E

  1. Launch

    This tiny helicopter rode to Mars on the Perseverance rover and landed on 18 February 2021.

  2. Discovery

    On 19 April 2021 it made the first powered flight on another planet. It was meant to fly 5 times and flew 72.

  3. Final chapter

    In January 2024 a rough landing damaged its rotor blades. NASA ended its mission, and it stays on Mars.

🔬 What it taught us: It proved that flying on Mars is possible, even though the air is about 100 times thinner than on Earth.
🚀 Space fact: It carried a tiny piece of fabric from the Wright brothers’ 1903 airplane.

Voyager 1

1977–today
Still sending data
Location: Interstellar space
Coordinates: Over 25 billion km from Earth

  1. Launch

    Launched on 5 September 1977 to visit Jupiter and Saturn.

  2. Discovery

    It found erupting volcanoes on Jupiter’s moon Io and, in 1990, took the “Pale Blue Dot” photo of Earth from billions of kilometres away.

  3. Final chapter

    In 2012 it became the first human-made object to enter interstellar space. It is still sending data home.

🔬 What it taught us: It showed scientists what the edge of the Sun’s bubble of particles is like, and it still measures the space between the stars.
🚀 Space fact: It carries a Golden Record with sounds and pictures from Earth. A radio signal now takes about a day to reach it.

Voyager 2

1977–today
Still sending data
Location: Interstellar space
Coordinates: Over 20 billion km from Earth

  1. Launch

    Launched on 20 August 1977, 16 days before Voyager 1, on a grand tour of the giant planets.

  2. Discovery

    It is the only spacecraft ever to visit Uranus (1986) and Neptune (1989), where it found new moons and rings.

  3. Final chapter

    It entered interstellar space in 2018 and is still sending data.

🔬 What it taught us: Its flybys revealed Uranus’s tilted magnetic field, Neptune’s fast winds and icy geysers on Triton.
🚀 Space fact: Its twin launched later but moved faster, so Voyager 1 is farther away.

Pioneer 10

1972–2003
Silent since 2003
Location: Deep space
Coordinates: Heading toward the star Aldebaran

  1. Launch

    Launched on 2 March 1972 to find out whether a spacecraft could cross the asteroid belt.

  2. Discovery

    It survived the belt and became the first spacecraft to fly past Jupiter in December 1973, measuring the planet’s dangerous radiation.

  3. Final chapter

    Its last signal reached Earth on 23 January 2003. It is still drifting through space, but too far away to hear.

🔬 What it taught us: It paved the way for later missions by showing that the asteroid belt and Jupiter’s radiation could be survived.
🚀 Space fact: It carries a gold plaque showing a man, a woman and Earth’s location, and will pass near Aldebaran in about 2 million years.
